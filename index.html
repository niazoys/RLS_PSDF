Uncertainty-aware Retinal Layer Segmentation through Probabilistic Signed Distance Functions

Abstract

We present a new approach for uncertainty-aware retinal layer segmentation in Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) scans using probabilistic signed distance functions (SDF). Traditional methods encounter difficulties in precise segmentation and lack geometrical grounding. To address these shortcomings, our methodology refines segmentation by predicting an SDF that parameterizes the retinal layer shape. We further enhance the framework by integrating probabilistic modeling, applying Gaussian distributions to encapsulate the uncertainty in the shape parameterization. This ensures a robust representation of retinal morphology even with ambiguous input or noise. Both quantitative and qualitative evaluations demonstrate superior performance when compared to other methods, offering a key step towards characterizing layer integrity for monitoring disease progression.

Method

Instead of classifying pixels or regressing points, we teach our model a continuous, geometric representation of the layer boundary. The model learns to output a Signed Distance Function (SDF), a map where every pixel's value is its distance to the boundary. The layer itself is the zero-level contour.

Crucially, we make this probabilistic. The network predicts not just one distance value, but a Gaussian distribution. This distribution is defined by a mean, $\mu(x,y)$, and a standard deviation, $\sigma(x,y)$, which tells us how certain the model is about the layer's location at every point.

$$p(d(x,y)|I,\theta) = \mathcal{N}(\mu(x,y), \sigma(x,y)^2)$$

Thanks to the Eikonal property of SDFs, this uncertainty in distance (represented by $\sigma$) directly translates to uncertainty in the layer's physical location, giving us a powerful tool for identifying ambiguous or pathological regions.

Results

Our SDF-based methods trace layers with far greater precision, especially in challenging cases with structural damage from disease. The model's uncertainty naturally increases in areas where the layer is disrupted or hard to see, acting as a potential biomarker for pathology.
